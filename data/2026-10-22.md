Abstract: Machine-learning surrogates in computational science often require not only accurate predictions but also expectations, variances, conserved integrals, and normalization constants. Conventional neural networks, including Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KANs), generally evaluate these quantities through Monte Carlo sampling or multidimensional quadrature, making repeated inference costly, particularly in high dimensions. We introduce KANs with Tractable Operators for Uncertainty and Constraints (TOUCKANs), whose finite-degree polynomial outer functions yield a decomposable sum--product representation. Under factorizing input measures, expectations and higher moments then reduce algebraically to deterministic recursions over one-dimensional leaf integrals. Independent and shared inner-block variants form a parameter-efficiency hierarchy, and a universal-approximation proof for its most restricted member extends to the complete class. The same deterministic operators allow conserved integrals and normalization to be imposed throughout training. Across uniform and Gaussian benchmarks through 50 dimensions, deterministic mean and variance errors remained below the median fixed-budget Monte Carlo errors in every case. TOUCKANs also conserved Cahn--Hilliard mass to machine precision and imposed normalization and orthogonality through deterministic moments, enabling joint computation of the lowest four Schrödinger eigenpairs in two, three, and five dimensions. TOUCKANs therefore connect KAN approximation with tractable probabilistic circuits, making statistical operators and integral constraints directly available within an expressive neural surrogate.

Abstract: We study an extremal regression problem, an example of multiple instance learning (MIL), where the predictors are given as multisets, called bags. The bag elements (called instances) possess hidden labels which are pooled to produce an observed bag-level label. Max pooling is common in MIL applications, but gives rise to vanishing gradients. To handle this, modern MIL pipelines approximate the max with a softmax, treating the sharpness as a learnable parameter, denoted $λ$. When the max pooling model is the true data generating process, learning will be successful when $λ$ is increased during gradient descent, thereby driving the dynamics into regions of parameter space with vanishing gradients. To understand the conditions under which gradient descent breaks down, we study a minimal model and derive a low dimensional projection of the gradient flow in the joint limit of large bags $n$ and high dimension $d$ with $α=\ln n/d$ fixed. The flow has two distinct phase transitions: an intrinsic freezing transition related to the random energy model of disordered systems, and a retrieval transition occurring in modern Hopfield networks. Numerical simulation confirms both the phase diagram and the convergence of the finite-size training gradient to its predicted asymptotic form.

Abstract: The success of convolutional neural networks is often attributed to locality and weight sharing. However, how these two properties separately shape learning, and how their effects depend on the spatial correlations of natural images, remain poorly understood. We address these questions in a teacher-student model with a convolutional teacher acting on $P$ input positions with correlation $c$ between positions. We compare convolutional and locally connected students trained by online stochastic gradient descent, and derive ordinary differential equations that describe their dynamics exactly in the limit of large feature dimension $D$. For uncorrelated positions ($c = 0$), the two students follow the same learning trajectory, with the convolutional student learning exactly $P$ times faster. We also find that locality affects the sample complexity: a student that matches the kernel size of the teacher recovers it from $\mathcal{O}_{D}(D)$ samples, whereas $\mathcal{O}_{D}(D\log(D))$ samples are needed with a larger kernel. For $c > 0$, spatial correlations can reduce the advantage of weight sharing. The asymptotic error of the convolutional student grows with $c$, whereas that of the locally connected student is independent of $c$ and overall lower when evaluated at the same learning rate. When the learning rate is instead tuned to the training horizon, however, the locally connected student wins at early times and the convolutional student at late times, with a transition that we predict analytically.

Abstract: Predicting molecular magnetism often requires electronic-structure calculations with spin-orbit coupling (SOC), which are costly to repeat across many molecules. The underlying electronic states can transform as spinors, yet equivariant surrogates typically use only integer-rank features. Inspired by the spinor nature of these electronic states, we develop eSpinor, an SU(2)-equivariant graph neural network that propagates spinor features. We initialize spinor features from eigenvectors of an approximate SOC Hamiltonian and use gauge-covariant message passing with a gauge-invariant readout to make predictions independent of the arbitrary eigenbasis choice. For a dysprosium magnetic resonance imaging probe, eSpinor improves real electron-spin correlations over a Hamiltonian-informed baseline despite similar static crystal-field errors and fewer parameters, while accurately predicting low-field nuclear relaxation in water and after transfer to methanol without retraining. Overall, these results demonstrate that SU(2)-equivariant spinor message passing is a useful inductive bias for molecular magnetism. We also find that similar static tensor errors can yield different dynamical errors, motivating model selection based on accuracy for the intended physical observables.
